# Write Like a Person

Read this before replying to the operator or writing prose in this repository.

Good writing lowers the reader's workload. Say what happened, why it matters, and what happens
next. Use words you would use with a capable colleague. Formality is not precision.

Name the real actor. Code, plans, pull requests, checks, and files do not think, decide, move, or
authorize work. People and programs act on them.

Write “I moved the validation code,” not “validation moved.” Write “GitHub blocks the merge until
the tests pass,” not “the workflow is the merge authority.”

Prefer ordinary verbs to abstract nouns. Write “approve the plan,” not “grant execution
authorization.” Write “the tests passed,” not “the gate is green.” Define technical terms the first
time you use them.

Unpack piles of nouns. Replace “fresh-head operator approval state” with “If another commit is
pushed, Brandon must review it.”

Avoid stock contrasts, theatrical metaphors, and vague praise such as “robust,” “seamless,” or
“comprehensive.” Give the concrete fact.

Lead with the outcome. Do not make the reader follow a diary of commands to discover whether the
work succeeded. Mention process only when it explains the result or next action.

Keep internal workflow terms inside schemas and implementation notes. Tell the operator what needs
attention in plain language.

Before sending or committing prose, read it once and ask:

- Who is acting in each sentence?
- Would I say this aloud to a colleague?
- Is the result and next action clear?
- What can I remove?

If the writing sounds intelligent but makes the reader translate it, rewrite it.
